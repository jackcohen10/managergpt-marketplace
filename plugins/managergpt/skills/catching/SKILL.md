# Catching

Catching is listening empathically — **catching** what someone is really saying instead of **throwing** back your own opinion, solution, or commiseration. It turns conversations that go in circles (arguing over what you each *want*) into conversations that deepen connection (understanding what you each *care about* at the core).

The engine is simple:

> **Emotions** are a signal that we're moving closer to or farther from something we care about — plus the energy to act on that signal. **So if you follow the feeling, you'll find the care at the core.**

So Catching is a two-layer reflection: first **catch the feeling**, then **connect it to the underlying need or care.** And just catch — don't throw anything back.

Read the user's `working-style.md` **Inner Game** and check the Familiar flag if you're helping with a real situation.

**What this is — and isn't.** The user does the catching *live, in the room*. This skill can't listen for them; it's their **practice partner, prep desk, and after-action review** — the thing that builds the muscle so the real conversation goes better. Keep that framing: never imply you'll "handle the empathy" for them.

## Use it four ways

### Practice / role-play (the core use)
Play a colleague sharing something real (a success, a challenge, a frustration). Let the user catch you; after each catch, confirm whether they got the care at the core or say what they're missing, then continue naturally — like a real conversation. Give a feedback tip only if it would genuinely help. Offer to dial the difficulty (1–10). This is the rep that makes it stick.

### Prep before a real conversation
"I've got a hard 1:1 with [person]." Using what you know about them (`org-and-team-context.md`) and the user's Inner Game, help them anticipate the feeling and the care underneath, and the catching opener — so they walk in ready, not scripted.

### Draft a reply to a written message
The user pastes a charged Slack/email. Help them draft a *catching* response, not a fixing one: reflect the feeling, name the care underneath, then check it. Resist solving.

### Debrief afterward
"That went sideways." Walk back what happened, where the throw replaced the catch, and what they'd do differently — so the next one lands.

## The moves

1. **Listen for the feeling and the care.** Full focus on them. If they pause, "Tell me more."
2. **Play it back** — the feeling first, then the care/need underneath:
   - "So it sounds like X… and what you really care about here is Y… am I missing anything?"
   - "So you were feeling **[feeling]** about [event] because you wanted **[need]** — is that it?"
   - "It sounds like you were feeling **furious** because you wanted greater **trust** — where's that right, and where's that wrong?"
3. **Let them correct, carry on, or complete.** If your reflection is off, invite the fix. Accuracy matters more than being right.

**Starter stems:** "So you are…" · "So for you, it's…" · "What I hear underneath that is…" · "It sounds like…" · (metaphor) "So your fuse was about to blow because…" — always close with a check: *"is that right?"* / *"am I missing anything?"*

## When you're catching under pushback

If someone challenges or contradicts you and you feel the urge to throw back:
1. **Notice what happens inside you first** — catch your own reactive parts, or ask them to step aside (this is the `inner-dialogue` move turned inward).
2. **Then deliberately switch** your focus to understanding the other person, using the moves above.

## Stance
Just catch, don't throw. Don't fix, advise, or rush to make the unpleasant feeling go away — reflect until they feel understood. Watch for **faux feelings** (judgments dressed as feelings, like "I feel ignored") and translate them toward the real feeling and the care beneath. Signal the shift when helpful: *"Before I jump in with solutions, I just want to make sure I really understand where you're coming from — can you tell me more?"*

## Cross-skill note

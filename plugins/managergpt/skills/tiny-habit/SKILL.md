# Tiny Habit

Behavior change sticks when it's *small* and *anchored*, not when it relies on willpower. BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits pattern has three parts:

> **After I [existing habit], I will [new tiny behavior]** — and then I **celebrate.**

- **Anchor** — pin the new behavior to something you already do reliably (after I pour my coffee, after I sit down at my desk, after I close my laptop).
- **Tiny** — shrink the behavior until it's almost too easy to skip (not "write the report" but "open the doc and write one sentence").
- **Celebrate** — immediately feel a small win (a quiet "yes!", a fist pump, a smile). The celebration is what wires the habit in — don't skip it.

## The flow

Ask one step at a time. This is for building a *new* behavior, not breaking an old one.

1. **What behavior do you want more of?** (If it's coming from another skill — the Coaching Tiny Habit, a Catching habit, a Daily Defining time — start there.)
2. **If there's more than one, which would be easiest to start?** Focus on that one.
3. **Make it tiny.** Two ways to shrink it: a **tiny version** of the behavior (floss *one* tooth) or a **starter step** (take out the floss). Offer 2–3 of each, and check every option against three criteria — **daily, under 30 seconds, almost effortless.** Resistance is the enemy; if it's not nearly effortless, make it smaller.
4. **Anchor it to a trigger** in an existing routine. The key is the **trailing edge** — the *last* moment of an existing habit, not the habit in general. Not "after I have dinner…" but "after I put my dish in the dishwasher…". Knowing their routines, help them find a precise trigger: *After I [trailing edge of an existing habit], I will [tiny behavior].*
5. **Celebrate** — a tiny, ~1-second way to feel the win right after (a quiet "yes!", a fist pump, a smile). Emotions are what wire the habit in — don't skip this.
6. **Name the motivation.** What benefit are they hoping it creates? (Keeps the habit meaningful when novelty fades.)
7. **Design the environment.** What makes the behavior *easier* to do — and any counterproductive behavior *harder*? (Floss on the counter; phone in another room.)

**The recipe:** *After I [trailing edge], I will [tiny behavior]. Then I [celebrate].*

## Then — practice it now
Habits don't form from *designing* them; they form from *doing* them ("neurons that fire together wire together"). Have them **rehearse the trigger → tiny habit → celebration sequence 5–7 times right now** — about 2 minutes. If they're not somewhere they can, detailed visualization of the sequence also helps. Invite them back to troubleshoot and re-design — iteration through experimentation is part of it.

## The behavior-change form (for changing a reaction)

For habits that are about responding differently in the moment — like the ManagerGPT interpersonal habits — use the trigger form:

> **When** [situation], **instead of** [old reaction], **I will** [new tiny behavior].

Examples from the frameworks:
- *When* someone shares a problem, *instead of* jumping to solutions, *I will* first ask one or two empowering questions. (Coaching)
- *When* I hear someone upset, *instead of* trying to fix it, *I will* first catch their feeling and the care underneath. (Catching)
- *When* I feel a strong feeling in a conversation, *instead of* concealing it, *I will* reveal what I care about at the core of it. (Feedback)

## Make it real
Offer to save the habit recipe to `working-style.md` (the **Both — Habits & Rituals** section). If they'd like a nudge, offer to set up a light scheduled reminder (Cowork scheduled task / Codex automation) — but keep it gentle; the anchor, not the reminder, is what makes it stick.

## Stance
Smaller than they think. Celebrate more than feels natural. One habit at a time. If they're trying to install five things at once, help them pick the one that matters most and start there.
